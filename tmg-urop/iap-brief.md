# UROP: Hold That Thought

Today, people use AI by taking turns: you prompt, wait, read the result, and prompt again. You can't grab an idea and nudge it while you watch it change.

In my upcoming [thesis](https://code.chuanqisun.com/master-thesis/), I want to give handles to ideas. What if we can provide simple controls for the meaning of the content generated by LLM or image models? A slider bound to "more poetic" or a puck that stands for one idea in a brainstorm. Handles can be on screen or physical. Either way, the output should change in real time while you move the handle, with no submit button, the way you mold clay with your hands.

I want to find out whether people working this way are more creative than with chat, feel more in control, and enjoy it more.

## Example projects (1–3 months each)

Let AI do the programming. Most of your time goes to design and research: reading papers, prototype ideas, designing and running a study, analysing the data, and writing it up.

Every project goes through design, build, and evaluation. By the end you should leave a long trail of working demos, study data, and reach the finish line with a short paper draft.

**1. Verb Palette**

- Design: Read about 30 systems that let people edit by meaning (DirectGPT, Textoshop, Cells/Generators/Lenses and others) and pull out a set of semantic "verbs" such as steer, blend, scale, and bind.
- Build: A text editor where each verb is a tool, used like a brush in Photoshop.
- Evaluate: Eight people do a writing task and think aloud. Which verbs do they use, and which do they ask for that don't exist yet?

**2. Semantic Dial**

- Design: An LLM suggests axes for a piece of writing, like cautious to bold or concrete to abstract.
- Build: Sliders that rewrite the text as you drag. Variants are generated ahead of time so the response is instant.
- Evaluate: About 12 people do the same task with the sliders and with a chat interface. Compare Creativity Support Index scores and sense of ownership. Do people feel they wrote the result?

**3. Idea Tokens**

- Design: Rules for combining ideas by position. Cards placed close together get blended, and a stacked card acts as a constraint on the one below. Try the rules out on about 6 people first.
- Build: A board of draggable idea cards, on screen or on a table, where the combined output updates as you move them.
- Evaluate: A brainstorming task against a chat baseline. Count distinct ideas, log interactions, and interview participants.

**4. Material Handles**

- Design: Give a semantic slider physical behaviour. It snaps to strong variants and gets sticky near clichés.
- Build: Add the behaviour to an on-screen slider, or to a force-feedback knob if you want to work with hardware.
- Evaluate: An A/B study with the behaviour on and off. Does it change how far people explore?

**5. Where Real Time Breaks**

- Design: A small toy where a sketch rewrites a sentence instantly, with a setting for added delay.
- Build: The toy, plus a delay layer that also plugs into my existing systems.
- Evaluate: Put people through delays from 50 ms to 5 s and find the point where it stops feeling like shaping and starts feeling like asking.

You can also propose your own project if it ties meaning to a handle and goes through all three stages.

## What I look for

- You can read papers quickly to extract original ideas and gain inspiration.
- You know some study design and statistics (R or Python), or want to learn. Qualitative coding is a plus.
- You write clearly and know how to de-slop AI output.
- You are a tool maker, not just a consumer. Bonus if you can show me a tool you made.
- HCI or cognitive science coursework helps but isn't required.
- Electronics and mechanical engineering experience a plus for physical projects.

## Risks

Design research can fail. A study can find no effect, and a prototype can turn out to be dull to use. Another research group can publish the same idea while you are still working. A company can drop a product that is better, faster than yours. Your work may never become a paper, but you can talk about it in a portfolio. You have to be OK with that.

Think from my perspective: if I can tell you exactly what to do, I would just ask AI to do it. So you'll plan your own work. I'll give you a question, an end point, and a weekly meeting. Deciding what to try next and what to drop is up to you. If you need a detailed task list to make progress, this UROP wouldn't work. If you've finished a self-directed project before, you'll probably do fine. In exchange, you own one research question from start to finish, and you'll be a co-author on any paper your work goes into.
