Tangible AI — Media Lab UROP Opening

Terms: 26 Fall · 27 IAP · 27 Spring

Interacting with AI should feel like a breath of fresh air.

Join us to design and build interactive systems that make us more human, and more than human.

Commitment: At least 8 dedicated hours every week.

Computation as human experience

The Tangible Media Group (TMG) at the MIT Media Lab, led by Professor Hiroshi Ishii, has spent over 30 years researching human-computer interaction by bridging the digital and physical worlds.

We are tired of screens, chat bots, and endless prompting. Why can't we grasp an agent and steer it in real-time?

We are working to increase the speed and bandwidth of human-machine interaction by 100-fold without brain-computer interfaces.

We want to help people achieve more by thinking more, intending more, acting more, and aspiring more.

We are the interaction maximalists.

What you will work on

You will help turn open-ended interaction questions into working prototypes, learn from what fails, and communicate what the team discovers. Depending on your strengths and the project direction, your work may include:

  • Design & storytelling: Collaborative brainstorming, whiteboarding, concept ideation, and crafting compelling visual and interaction narratives.
  • Real-time interaction systems: Engineering low-latency speech, gesture, vision, and robotic interaction loops for human-computer collaboration.
  • Computer vision & multimodal models: Integrating, training, and fine-tuning models for spatial tracking, object recognition, and interactive systems.
  • Programming tools & abstractions: Designing domain-specific languages and interfaces that let people express intent and directly shape behavior.
  • Hardware & embedded systems: Designing and fabricating custom hardware, embedded circuits, and tangible physical objects.
  • Research & dissemination: Documenting experiments, explaining design decisions, and developing work toward conference publications or open-source software projects.

You do not need to arrive with every skill on this list. We are a team of diverse talents with complementary strengths. Bring something you can contribute and an appetite for learning the rest.

An early-stage team. Real ownership.

This is a new UROP with the pace and ambiguity of an early-stage startup. We are building an airplane while flying it: the problem space is not fully mapped out, the team structure is evolving, and we may change direction when an approach does not work.

You will not inherit a settled roadmap or a queue of daily assignments. You will help shape the questions, propose next steps, and keep making progress when the answer is unclear.

Are you still early in your research career? You will receive technical mentorship from peers, coaching on unfamiliar skills, and career guidance. In return, you own execution: surface problems early, ask for help, and bring ideas for moving forward.

How we work together

Ownership > instruction

Proactively identify problems and propose solutions. We build to think: sketch, prototype, test, and use what you learn to decide what comes next. Waiting for a daily task assignment will leave too much of this opportunity unused.

Peer learning > working alone

Teach what you know and learn what you do not. Helping a teammate get unstuck, documenting a discovery, and sharing a useful tool are part of the work—not distractions from your own progress. The team succeeds when everyone gets stronger.

Growth mindset > ego

Conflicts and failures are inevitable. Challenge ideas with respect, celebrate others' success, and treat your own failures as data. Feedback goes both ways: you are expected to question decisions and call out mistakes, including your mentor's.

8 dedicated hours, every week

You need a strict minimum of 8 dedicated hours every week. Not 8 hours of late-night cramming. It includes weekly stand-up meetings, ad hoc in-person and online syncs, documentation writing, helping your teammates get unstuck, and moving your own tasks forward. Others' progress depends on your availability, so please avoid signing up if your current semester is too tight. Take care of what's most important to you, and join us in the future.

Team culture

We make time to get to know one another outside the lab: hiking, skiing, grabbing food, sailing, and visiting museums. We support and celebrate each other both at work and in life.

Who this is for

You may feel at home here if

  • You ask why to build something, not just how or what to build.
  • You enjoy the ideas behind Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs (SICP), or thinking deeply about programming abstractions.
  • You have designed, CAD modeled, or 3D printed physical objects, or enjoy creating art with code and building interactive media.
  • You can take initiative without pretending to know everything, and are willing to ask questions, share unfinished work, and change your mind.
  • You want to use AI critically: to extend your abilities while remaining responsible for understanding and evaluating what you build.

This may not be the right UROP if

  • You need a well-specified project with clear instructions and direct mentorship from graduate students.
  • You are looking for a low-effort credit filler or cannot protect the weekly commitment.
  • You prefer working alone to teaching, learning, and sharing responsibility.
  • You want to offload all problem-solving to AI without understanding the underlying mechanics.
  • You are only interested in theoretical machine learning research without building working physical or software artifacts.

A note from Stack

What you will walk away with

This UROP is designed to help you develop:

  • Work you can explain and show: Portfolio projects that connect research questions, design decisions, technical implementation, and lessons learned.
  • Industry-relevant engineering habits: Experience building, shipping, and communicating as a team, including version control, testing, software architecture, documentation, and CI/CD.
  • Judgment under uncertainty: Practice breaking down ambiguous problems, choosing useful experiments, learning from failure, and adapting a plan.
  • Personal AI tools & workflows: Practical ways to extend your abilities that remain useful beyond this UROP.
  • Collaboration & leadership: Experience teaching peers, receiving feedback, resolving disagreements, and helping a team move forward.
  • Career perspective & community: Mentorship informed by industry experience, and connections with researchers, designers, and engineers across the Media Lab.

We aim to build work worth publishing and sharing. Publication may take the form of open-source release, social media posting, and conference/journal submission. Faculty-advised publication is not guaranteed. When we publish, authorship and credit will reflect actual intellectual contributions. Your mentor will advocate for first or co-first authorship when your contribution merits it.

What to submit when applying

If this sounds like the kind of team you want to help build, send your resume/portfolio and answers to the following four questions:

  1. Goals: What skills do you want to build during this UROP, and what goals are you pursuing that this project supports?
  2. Projects: Describe something cool or significant you have made. It can be hardware, software, art, or design. What did you contribute, and what could you teach a teammate from that experience?
  3. Reflections on failure: Share a mistake or technical failure from a past project. What happened, and what would you do differently now?
  4. AI usage: How do you currently use AI tools in your workflow? What works well, and what do you find frustrating or limiting?

Also include your preferred term or terms and how you plan to make room for at least 8 dedicated hours each week. Please read the expectations above before applying. We will get back to you by email.
