Abstract

The enduring joy of programming arises from three foundational experiences: intimate contact with computational material, the sense of control gained by grabbing handles that manipulate system semantics, and the deep confidence that comes from comprehending every part of a running system. Today, the rapid rise of generative media, spanning neural image synthesis, dynamic simulations, and agentic code generation, threatens to extinguish this joy. Although generative artificial intelligence accelerates raw production, prevailing interfaces restrict creators to turn-based natural language prompting and autonomous delegation. This conversational bottleneck severs continuous sensory-motor contact with the underlying model, widens the Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation, induces severe cognitive debt, and reduces creative practice to detached verification. This thesis investigates the hypothesis that direct manipulation can restore the joy of programming to generative media. To bridge the gap between high-dimensional generative models and embodied human action, I propose a neuro-symbolic interaction framework that synthesizes Ben Shneiderman's direct manipulation with Pattie Maes's interface agents into a Fast-and-Slow loop. In the Slow analytical loop, multimodal interface agents interpret human speech, gesture, and hand-drawn ink to synthesize bespoke programmatic handles and inspectable rules on the fly. In the Fast sensory-motor loop, creators grab these spatial, parametric, and tangible handles to sculpt continuous solution spaces in real time. I evaluate this hypothesis through a progressive portfolio of five systems: Dream Brush (spatial-semantic diffusion control via physical water calligraphy), Parametric Diffusion (real-time steering of generative diffusion via reactive cellular substrates), Sable (a projection-augmented granular sandbox for 3D semantic sculpting), Braindump (a speech-and-gesture system for human-to-machine mental model transfer), and Papershop (a communal paper workbench uniting hand-drawn simulations with interface agents). Through qualitative user studies and design critiques exploring creative flow, semantic and articulatory distance, mental model alignment, and creative ownership, this work establishes a blueprint for generative media environments that prioritize human agency and joyful craft.

1 Introduction

Programming at its best is a deeply joyful creative medium. This joy stems from three interconnected pillars: the tactile satisfaction of remaining in continuous contact with computational material (such as logical building blocks, components, and data structures), the empowering sense of control when manipulating semantic handles to steer system behavior, and the confidence of full comprehension that allows a creator to adapt a system to evolving needs [1], [7], [27]. However, the contemporary shift toward generative media, encompassing both neural audiovisual synthesis and agentic software creation, has created a troubling paradox. Empirical studies show that while generative models increase raw productivity, creators and developers feel increasingly alienated from their work [18], [19], [29]. Advait Sarkar identifies this alienation as material disengagement: by mediating creation through conversational text prompts and autonomous agents, existing tools sever the creator's direct contact with code and media state [20]. Turn-based chat interfaces reintroduce the latency of ahead-of-time batch processing, inflating semantic and articulatory distance across the Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation [2], [21]. Stripped of rich manipulation surfaces and immediate feedback loops, practitioners suffer from prompt fatigue, context-switching exhaustion, and accumulating cognitive debt. To preserve the joy of programming in the era of generative media, we require an interaction paradigm that places the human creator back into continuous, hands-on contact with the computational model.

Restoring this continuous contact is essential for sustaining human creativity, psychological flow, and epistemological diversity. In his philosophy of creative practice, Tim Ingold critiques the hylomorphic model of making, wherein a creator stands outside passive matter and imposes a preconceived verbal or mental form upon it [10]. Prompt-driven generative media enforces this exact hylomorphic detachment. In contrast, Ingold describes the textility of making, where form grows through an improvisational, sensory-motor correspondence between a sentient practitioner and an active material that possesses its own structural tendencies and flows [10], [11]. This material correspondence echoes Sherry Turkle and Seymour Papert's call for epistemological pluralism, which celebrates proximal, concrete tinkering (bricolage) as a vital mode of computational thought [12], as well as the live coding and creative coding traditions championed by practitioners who treat algorithms as expressive audiovisual instruments [9], [13], [14], [17]. When a programming environment provides immediate, unambiguous feedback and perceived control, the creator enters Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's psychological flow state, where action and awareness merge into autotelic enjoyment [16], [28]. Bringing these tangible, continuous qualities to generative media ensures that artists, programmers, and end-users retain creative ownership and deep comprehension over the systems they build [25], [30].

This thesis advances the core hypothesis that direct manipulation can resolve the alienation of generative media and restore the joy of programming. Because generative models operate over vast, high-dimensional latent and algorithmic spaces, applying classic direct manipulation requires a new synthesis between Ben Shneiderman's vision of continuous, reversible physical actions [1] and Pattie Maes's vision of intelligent interface agents [3]. I formulate this synthesis as a Fast-and-Slow neuro-symbolic sculpting process. In the Slow analytical loop, multimodal interface agents observe human speech, depictive gestures, and freehand sketches to perform on-the-fly program synthesis, generating custom, inspectable control structures and generative user interfaces [4], [22], [67]. In the Fast sensory-motor loop, the creator uses these tangible, spatial, and parametric handles to directly manipulate computational substrates and explore continuous solution spaces in real time [5], [6], [62]. I investigate and validate this hypothesis across five progressive systems: Dream Brush [31], Parametric Diffusion (sh.elf and life.config), Sable, Braindump, and Papershop. Spanning calligraphic canvases, reactive cellular grids, granular 3D sandboxes, mid-air gestural capture, and communal paper desks, this portfolio demonstrates how direct manipulation transforms generative media from an opaque oracle into a joyful, sculptable medium.

2 Contribution

Through the theoretical formulation, engineering implementation, and qualitative evaluation of Direct Manipulation for Generative Media, my goal is to make four key contributions:

  • Five-System Portfolio for Direct Manipulation of Generative Media: Design, fabrication, and implementation of five interactive systems that instantiate direct manipulation across visual, tangible, and programmatic generative media: (1) Dream Brush, a calligraphic water canvas unifying symbolic prompts with spatial stroke control; (2) Parametric Diffusion (sh.elf and life.config), a real-time system steering generative diffusion via reactive cellular substrates; (3) Sable, a projection-augmented granular sandbox for bimanual 3D semantic sculpting; (4) Braindump, a speech-and-gesture system for human-to-machine mental model transfer; and (5) Papershop, a social paper-computing workbench integrating hand-drawn simulations with interface agents.
  • Open-Source Neuro-Symbolic Toolkits and Conditioning Bridges: Creation of reusable software libraries and accessible hardware calibration guides, including a unified framework for real-time gesture and object tracking coupled with a generative rendering loop, an overhead depth-to-conditioning pipeline for tangible sandboxes, and an open-source multimodal agent runtime for projection-mapped paper programs optimized for everyday consumer hardware.
  • Qualitative Insights into Flow, Agency, and Mental Model Synchronization: Qualitative findings and observational insights from studio critiques, think-aloud sessions, and exploratory user studies examining how direct manipulation of generative media shapes creative flow, agency, semantic and articulatory distance, and system comprehension compared to conversational prompting workflows.
  • Design Recommendations for Joyful Generative Programming Environments: A cohesive set of design principles for future programming languages, IDEs, and creative runtimes that balance fast sensory-motor manipulation with slow agentic program synthesis, proportional effort-to-fidelity sculpting, and transparent concept design.

By open-sourcing all software runtimes and consumer-accessible hardware specifications, this work provides the HCI, programming languages, and creative coding communities with practical foundations for building human-centric generative tools.

3 Related Work

My thesis builds upon four foundational pillars of literature: the cognitive mechanics of direct manipulation and mental models, tangible user interfaces, creative coding as material craft, and mixed-initiative interface agents.

3.1 Direct Manipulation, Instrumental Interaction, and Mental Models

Ben Shneiderman coined the term direct manipulation in 1983 to explain why certain interactive systems generate immediate user delight, mastery, and confidence [1]. By presenting a continuous visual representation of objects of interest and replacing complex command syntax with rapid, incremental, reversible physical actions, direct manipulation provides an uninterrupted sensory-motor loop. Hutchins, Hollan, and Norman grounded this paradigm in cognitive science, showing that direct manipulation bridges the Gulf of Execution and the Gulf of Evaluation by minimizing semantic distance (how closely system semantics match human intent) and articulatory distance (how directly the physical form of an action reflects its meaning) [2]. Michel Beaudouin-Lafon expanded these principles to post-WIMP interfaces through Instrumental Interaction [32] and the design patterns of reification, polymorphism, and reuse [33], [34], [35], demonstrating that turning abstract commands into first-class manipulable objects minimizes spatial and temporal indirection.

In programming system design, Bret Victor frames human-computer interaction as the continuous exchange of information between a human mental model and a computer model [7], [36]. Through Learnable Programming and Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction, Victor demonstrates that deep comprehension requires making execution state visible, linking code directly to concrete visual behavior, and providing interactive handles to scrub across time and continuous parameter spaces [7], [8], [9]. Evaluated against Green and Petre's Cognitive Dimensions of Notation [26] and Daniel Jackson's concept design framework [27], current conversational generative media tools fail because they hide dependencies inside stochastic black boxes and impose high viscosity when users attempt fine-grained adjustments.

3.2 Tangible Bits, Graspable Handles, and Augmented Paper Workbenches

To engage the full richness of human kinesthetic and spatial intelligence [37], Hiroshi Ishii and Brygg Ullmer introduced Tangible Bits, coupling digital bits with physical atoms across interactive surfaces [6]. Grounded in Fitzmaurice, Ishii, and Buxton's Bricks [5], tangible user interfaces (TUIs) replace sequential, time-multiplexed GUI inputs with space-multiplexed physical handles that support two-handed parallel manipulation. Ullmer and Ishii's MCRpd architecture formalizes how physical representations (Rp) and projected digital representations (Rd) synchronize with an underlying computational model (M) [38].

A rich lineage of MIT Tangible Media Group systems demonstrates the power of coupling physical materials with live computation. I/O Brush turned everyday physical textures and movements into drawing ink [39], while Urp coupled architectural scale models on a workbench to real-time wind and shadow simulations [40]. Most relevant to my thesis, Illuminating Clay and SandScape pioneered Continuous Plastic TUIs, using overhead depth sensing and projection over deformable clay and glass beads to enable bimanual topographic sculpting [41], [42], a paradigm further popularized by SARndbox [43] and critiqued for parametric extensibility by Philip Belesky [44]. Later visions including Radical Atoms and inFORM explored dynamically actuated physical constraints [45], [46]. Concurrently, Pierre Wellner's DigitalDesk [47], François Guimbretière's PADD [48], Ishii and Kobayashi's ClearBoard [49], and Bret Victor's communal Dynamicland (Realtalk) [50], [51] established that augmenting physical paper on shared tables preserves gaze awareness and fosters embodied facilitation [52]. My work extends these tangible and paper-based architectures into the domain of steerable generative media and neuro-symbolic synthesis.

3.3 Creative Coding, Live Performance, and Generative Substrates

In creative practice, computation serves as an expressive medium where the joy of programming manifests through live audiovisual improvisation. Zach Lieberman's openFrameworks empowers artists with transparent, low-level C++ building blocks that keep the creator close to pixel buffers, audio streams, and hardware sensors [17]. Similarly, the live coding movement treats source code as a performative instrument modified in real time [13], [15]. In TidalCycles, Alex McLean shows how functional programming simplicity, representing musical patterns as pure composable functions of cyclic time, enables rapid reversible changes that induce psychological flow [14], [28]. Emma Cocker describes this live coding state as meletē, a meditative thinking-in-action that balances deliberate analytical framing with immediate performative response [16].

This creative coding ethos embodies Tim Ingold's textility of making, where the artist follows the active forces and flows of a material [10], [11]. While early cybernetic art systems like Harold Cohen's AARON operated autonomously [53] and Sougwen Chung's D.O.U.G. explored robotic co-drawing [54], [55], modern visual generative media relies heavily on latent diffusion. Recent computer vision breakthroughs have opened the door to spatial and real-time diffusion control: ControlNet [56], SpaText [57], MultiDiffusion [58], Blended Latent Diffusion [59], Make-A-Scene [60], T2I-Adapter [61], FreeControl [62], and DragGAN [63] enable localized spatial and manifold conditioning, while StreamDiffusion and StreamDiffusionV2 demonstrate interactive frame rates via stream batching and residual classifier-free guidance [64], [65]. By uniting low-latency cloud and streaming diffusion pipelines with biological morphogenesis models, specifically Alan Turing's reaction-diffusion equations [66], [67] and Mordvintsev's Growing Neural Cellular Automata [68], my thesis provides generative media with a living, directly manipulable computational substrate.

3.4 Interface Agents, Multimodal Dialogue, and Neuro-Symbolic Synthesis

Bringing direct manipulation to general-purpose generative media and end-user programming requires addressing the classic 1997 debate between Ben Shneiderman and Pattie Maes regarding direct manipulation versus interface agents [3]. While Shneiderman emphasized user control and predictability, Maes demonstrated that interface agents can overcome end-user programming friction by inferring intent and automating complex syntactic plumbing. Foundational work in Programming by Demonstration (PBD) and Programming by Example (PBE) by Allen Cypher [23] and Henry Lieberman [24] proved that agents can synthesize generalized procedures directly from concrete user actions on visual objects. In programming languages, Armando Solar-Lezama's Sketch formalized neuro-symbolic synthesis, allowing humans to specify high-level structural sketches while an inductive solver synthesizes low-level parameters [22].

Empirical studies of modern AI coding by Shraddha Barke et al. reveal a bimodal workflow: developers switch between a fast acceleration mode and a slow exploration mode [18]. Damien Masson et al.'s DirectGPT bridges these modes by turning natural language prompts into reusable direct manipulation tool buttons that act on spatial selections [4]. Furthermore, to transfer human mental models into the machine without text-prompting friction, we can leverage multimodal speech and gesture systems pioneered at MIT: Richard Bolt's "Put-That-There" [69] and two-handed kinematic dialogue [70], Koons and Sparrell's Iconic depictive gestures [71], Philip Cohen's synergistic blending of direct manipulation and natural language [72], Cheyer's Open Agent Architecture [73], and interactive machine learning loops [74], [75]. Contemporary systems such as Ken Perlin's Chalktalk [76], DrawTalking [77], and Ink & Switch's Potluck and Inkbase [25], [78], [79] validate that freehand ink and spoken rules can dynamically construct live simulations. My thesis unifies these multimodal agent techniques with tangible direct manipulation to create a cohesive generative media workbench.

4 Proposed Work

4.1 System Overview: Testing the Direct Manipulation Hypothesis

To systematically test how direct manipulation can restore the joy of programming across generative media, I propose a portfolio of five systems organized around a Fast-and-Slow neuro-symbolic sculpting workflow. Across all five systems, the creator begins with low-effort, general gestures to establish coarse structure and progressively refines the work toward high-fidelity specificity, ensuring that physical and cognitive effort scales proportionally with visual and behavioral fidelity [30].

The portfolio progresses across three levels of generative media complexity:

  1. Level 1: Spatial and Parametric Direct Manipulation of Visual Generative Media (Completed). Dream Brush and Parametric Diffusion replace detached text-to-image prompting with continuous spatial calligraphy and reactive cellular substrates, establishing an immediate Fast sensory-motor loop.
  2. Level 2: Tangible 3D Sculpting and Multimodal Mental Model Transfer (In Progress). Sable merges Dream Brush and Parametric Diffusion inside a physical granular sandbox for bimanual 3D world sculpting. Concurrently, Braindump establishes the Slow neuro-symbolic loop, using speech and depictive two-handed gestures to transfer human conceptual models to the machine and generate interactive handles on the fly.
  3. Level 3: Integrated Neuro-Symbolic Programming on Physical Paper (Culminating Prototype). Papershop unites the Fast direct manipulation loop and the Slow interface agent loop on an augmented communal desk, allowing users to sketch on physical paper to perform simulations, visual compositions, and collaborative knowledge sharing.

4.2 Research Questions

  1. Redefining Programming and Interaction in Generative Media: How can a Fast-and-Slow neuro-symbolic architecture reconcile direct manipulation (Shneiderman) with interface agents (Maes) so that human mental models and generative computer models remain in continuous perceptual synchronization?
  2. Restoring the Three Pillars of Joy: How does manipulating generative media through tangible, spatial, and reactive substrates (Dream Brush, Parametric Diffusion, Sable) affect a creator's experience of material contact, control, comprehension, and creative flow compared to conversational prompting?
  3. Proportional Effort-to-Fidelity Sculpting: How effectively do multi-resolution physical and cellular substrates allow creators to sculpt generative media from rapid, coarse conceptual blocking to fine-grained semantic and structural refinement?
  4. End-User & Social Programmability: How does combining freehand paper sketching with multimodal speech-and-gesture interface agents (Braindump, Papershop) lower end-user programming friction while preserving creative ownership and social legibility?

4.3 Technical Details and System Architecture

  • Hardware Platforms (Optimized for Off-the-Shelf Consumer Electronics):
    • Dream Brush: Water-sensitive calligraphy canvas, traditional brush, standard overhead RGB webcam, and consumer short-throw projector.
    • Parametric Diffusion (sh.elf & life.config): Standard personal computer connected to off-the-shelf cloud-hosted text-to-image and image-to-image diffusion models, paired with a multi-touch surface and tangible rotary/slider control console.
    • Sable: Custom-fabricated sandbox filled with fine white glass beads, overhead depth sensor, standard RGB webcam for calligraphic stroke tracking, and consumer projector.
    • Braindump: Built entirely with off-the-shelf personal computers, built-in microphones, and standard consumer webcams, requiring no specialized depth or optical tracking hardware.
    • Papershop: Shared wooden workbench, standard off-the-shelf consumer webcam and projector rig with ArUco tag fiducial registration, physical paper sheets, and standard felt-tip pens.
  • Software Stack & Core Technologies:
    • Consumer-Accessible Vision & Gesture Tracking: All systems are engineered to run on everyday consumer hardware for maximum accessibility and availability. Real-time hand, body, and gesture tracking rely heavily on MediaPipe alongside lightweight fine-tuned YOLO object tracking models, complemented by SIMD-accelerated WebAssembly (WASM) for CPU-based computer vision tasks (such as contour extraction and ArUco fiducial registration).
    • WebGPU Substrates & Cloud Generative Rendering Loop: Client-side WebGPU compute shaders run responsive 2D reaction-diffusion [66] and cellular substrates locally in the browser, coupled to a real-time generative rendering loop powered by off-the-shelf cloud-hosted text-to-image and image-to-image diffusion APIs.
    • Frontier Multimodal Agent & Synthesis Runtime: Rather than training custom deep neural networks or bespoke LLMs, the Slow synthesis loop leverages off-the-shelf frontier lab models (such as Gemini 4 and GPT-6.1) for speech, sketch, and gesture interpretation, driving a TypeScript/React reactive dataflow runtime (inspired by Inkbase [79] and Potluck [78]).
  • Current Completion Status: Dream Brush (100% complete; published at ACM DIS '26 Companion [31]); Parametric Diffusion (100% complete; sh.elf and life.config deployed); Papershop (30% complete; initial tabletop prototype with webcam ArUco registration and ink tracking); Sable (20% complete; initial sandbox ideation and depth-to-diffusion conditioning tests, fabrication scheduled for January); Braindump (20% complete; multimodal interaction grammar specified, webcam + MediaPipe software prototyping scheduled for December).

4.4 Detailed System Designs and User Workflows

1. Dream Brush: Unifying Symbolic Intent and Spatial Gesture

In conventional controllable image generation (such as SpaText [57] or Make-A-Scene [60]), the user's workflow is fractured across two incompatible modalities: drawing a spatial mask with a mouse and typing a symbolic text prompt on a keyboard. Dream Brush [31] resolves this fracture through the medium of Chinese calligraphy, where written characters carry both semantic meaning and expressive spatial form. As a creator writes characters with water on a physical canvas, an overhead camera captures the wet brushstroke's geometry while a vision-language pipeline identifies the character's semantic concept. Using spatial conditioning [56] and Blended Latent Diffusion [59], the system synthesizes visual motifs that follow the exact calligraphic brushwork and blend seamlessly into the existing projected painting. Evaluated through Beaudouin-Lafon's Instrumental Interaction framework [32], the calligraphy brush achieves near-zero spatial and temporal indirection, transforming prompt engineering into embodied mark-making.

2. Parametric Diffusion: Computational Substrates as Semantic Handles

To achieve true direct manipulation in visual generative media, the system must respond to user input with immediate, continuous, and reversible visual feedback [1]. Standard turn-based diffusion interfaces force users to wait between isolated text prompts, breaking the sensory-motor loop. Parametric Diffusion (implemented across sh.elf and life.config) solves this by inserting a real-time computational substrate between the human creator and off-the-shelf cloud-hosted text-to-image and image-to-image diffusion models. Inspired by Alan Turing's chemical morphogenesis [67], Greg Turk's reaction-diffusion textures [66], and cellular automata [68], the substrate is a 2D dynamical system running in WebGPU. Using multi-touch gestures and tangible dials on a standard personal computer, the creator directly perturbs cell states and adjusts morphogenetic parameters (feed rates, diffusion gradients, vector flows). Because these cellular patterns continuously drive the generative rendering loop via image-to-image conditioning [56], [62], the creator can grab semantic handles to bend, grow, and heal generative imagery, experiencing the latent space as an active, self-organizing material [10].

3. Sable: Bimanual 3D Semantic Sculpting in a Tangible Sandbox

Sable elevates the 2D interactions of Dream Brush and Parametric Diffusion into a 3D Continuous Plastic TUI modeled on Illuminating Clay [41] and SandScape [42]. Standing at a projection-augmented sandbox filled with fine glass beads, the creator uses both hands concurrently (Bricks [5]) to sculpt physical topography. An overhead depth sensor captures the 3D surface elevation and normal maps, which drive a live WebGPU cellular hydrodynamic and reaction-diffusion substrate flowing over the terrain. Simultaneously, the creator can use a finger to draw calligraphic symbols or boundary paths directly into the sand (Dream Brush). The generative rendering loop fuses the 3D topographic depth, the evolving cellular substrate, and the localized symbolic strokes via cloud-hosted image-to-image diffusion to project coherent, living worlds directly onto the physical sand. Broad, sweeping hand movements rapidly reshape macro-level geography (low effort, coarse fidelity), while delicate finger pinching carves intricate architectural or ecological features (high effort, high fidelity), fulfilling the sculpting process.

4. Braindump: Multimodal Mental Model Transfer and Handle Synthesis

While Dream Brush, Parametric Diffusion, and Sable provide fast sensory-motor handles for visual generative media, general-purpose programming requires constructing structural and behavioral models. In Magic Ink [36], Bret Victor demonstrated how software can transfer knowledge from the machine to the human mental model through context-sensitive graphics. Braindump addresses the complementary challenge: transferring a complex mental model from the human mind into the computer without the articulatory bottleneck of typing code or text prompts.

Drawing on Richard Bolt's "Put-That-There" [69], two-handed gesture dialogue [70], and Koons and Sparrell's Iconic depictive gestures [71], Braindump allows a creator to externalize ideas through synchronized speech and mid-air bimanual gestures using only an off-the-shelf webcam and a standard personal computer. As the creator speaks about a system's architecture while using both hands to pantomime shapes, spatial topologies, and kinematic interactions, MediaPipe and SIMD-accelerated WASM vision pipelines track the hand trajectories and feed them alongside speech transcripts to frontier multimodal LLMs (such as Gemini 4 or GPT-6.1). Crucially, the agent's output is an inspectable concept graph [27] equipped with custom direct manipulation affordances (sliders, draggable spatial nodes, and scrubbable state timelines) [4], [8]. Once the Slow agentic loop reifies these handles onto the canvas, the creator immediately enters the Fast loop, grabbing the handles to test edge cases, verify system comprehension, and refine the underlying logic.

5. Papershop: Uniting Direct Manipulation and Interface Agents on Paper

Papershop represents the culminating system of the thesis, integrating direct manipulation and interface agents into a communal, paper-based programming environment built entirely from off-the-shelf consumer electronics. Building upon Pierre Wellner's DigitalDesk [47] and Bret Victor's Dynamicland [50], Papershop uses a standard consumer webcam, ArUco fiducial tags, and an off-the-shelf projector above a shared wooden table so multiple creators can program together using ordinary paper and pens while maintaining natural eye contact (ClearBoard [49]). Where Dynamicland requires users to type textual code onto paper rulecards and explicitly rejects AI agents, Papershop demonstrates that multimodal interface agents powered by frontier LLMs can turn hand-drawn ink sketches directly into live, malleable software [25], [77].

In Papershop, a user sketches a visual diagram on paper, such as a mechanical linkage, a cellular automaton grid, or a musical sequencer, and uses deictic speech and pointing (Braindump) to specify behavior: "Make this gear drive that piston, and let me control the torque with this drawn dial." Within moments (the Slow synthesis loop), the Papershop runtime—combining SIMD-accelerated WASM computer vision, fine-tuned YOLO object tracking, MediaPipe hand tracking, and frontier LLMs (Gemini 4 / GPT-6.1)—segments the ink strokes into reified objects [33], synthesizes a constraint-based reactive program (Sketch [22], Inkbase [79]), and projects live physical motion, dataflow wires, and interactive UI overlays directly onto the physical paper. Entering the Fast manipulation loop, collaborators can slide physical paper dials, draw new ink strokes that immediately participate in the live simulation, or place two separate ArUco-tagged sheets of paper side by side to link their computational models together. All synthesized rules and state variables are projected as transparent inline annotations next to the user's ink (Potluck [78]), guaranteeing full system comprehension and eliminating cognitive debt.

4.5 Design Process

My design process grounds Hiroshi Ishii's vision-driven design methodology [80] in iterative studio critiques with calligraphers, creative coders, and domain experts. A guiding principle across all five systems is preserving the authentic tactile resistance and spatial affordances of physical media while relying on accessible, off-the-shelf consumer hardware. During the development of Dream Brush, early prototypes using digital styluses on tablet screens felt sterile and disconnected; transitioning to real water on a mineral-coated fabric canvas restored the physical drag, bristle splay, and evaporative timing that calligraphers rely upon for expressive control [31]. Similarly, for Sable and Papershop, all sensing and projection hardware consists of standard webcams and consumer projectors housed in an overhead frame. Keeping the physical sand and paper completely free of tethered sensors or head-mounted displays ensures a zero-barrier entry point, inviting spontaneous multi-user tinkering and embodied facilitation [52], [81].

4.6 User Studies & Qualitative Evaluation

  • Completed Pilot Studies: In our published evaluation of Dream Brush (DIS '26 [31]), qualitative studio sessions with practicing artists confirmed that unifying symbolic character writing with spatial stroke conditioning eliminated prompt-formulation interruptions and fostered a sustained state of creative correspondence with the diffusion model. In addition, interactive installations of Parametric Diffusion (sh.elf and life.config) demonstrated that coupling responsive cellular substrates with cloud-hosted diffusion shifts user perception from "waiting for an AI generation" to "physically sculpting a live medium."
  • Proposed Qualitative Evaluation Design: To evaluate my hypothesis across the complete portfolio, I will conduct two qualitative user studies and design critique sessions:
    • Study 1: Direct Manipulation of Visual Generative Media (Sable & Parametric Diffusion). An exploratory studio study with visual artists, designers, and creative coders. Participants will engage in both a targeted composition task and an open-ended world-sculpting session across three workflows:
      • Workflow A (Conversational + 2D GUI Baseline): Standard text prompting paired with mouse-drawn 2D conditioning maps.
      • Workflow B (2D Parametric Substrate Direct Manipulation): Multi-touch and tangible dial manipulation of Parametric Diffusion cellular substrates.
      • Workflow C (3D Tangible Direct Manipulation - Sable): Bimanual physical sand sculpting and calligraphic sand strokes in the Sable sandbox.
    • Study 2: Neuro-Symbolic Direct Manipulation for Programming (Braindump & Papershop). A qualitative study with end-user domain experts and experienced programmers, observed in both solo and paired collaborative sessions. Participants will build interactive physics simulations and custom data calculation tools across two environments:
      • Environment A (Agentic Chat Coding Baseline): Creating interactive applets via a conversational LLM coding interface.
      • Environment B (Neuro-Symbolic Direct Manipulation - Papershop + Braindump): Freehand paper sketching combined with speech/gesture handle synthesis and direct manipulation on the augmented workbench.
  • Qualitative Data Collection & Analysis:
    • Observational & Think-Aloud Protocols: Multi-angle video recordings, concurrent and retrospective think-aloud protocols, and artifact walkthroughs capturing how creators transition between the Slow agentic synthesis loop and the Fast direct manipulation loop.
    • Semi-Structured Interviews & Thematic Analysis: In-depth exit interviews and studio critiques will be analyzed via reflexive thematic analysis, examining participants' lived experience of the three pillars of joy (material contact, perceived control, and system comprehension), alongside creative ownership, semantic and articulatory distance [2], material correspondence [10], mental model alignment [7], and collaborative dynamics [52].
