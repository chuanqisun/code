Abstract

Near-future AI will be real time. Generative models already approach interactive frame rates, yet people still work with AI in turns: they prompt, wait, and judge. These turns make AI indirect, because intent must pass through a prompt; pliant, because the model yields to whatever it is asked; and monistic, because its outputs converge on one answer. Hold That Thought proposes S3, an architecture that connects three nodes: the somatic, the symbolic, and the semantic. Direct Manipulation linked the body to symbols. Interface Agents linked the body to meaning but skipped symbols. S3 routes the body through symbols bound to meaning, so that a hand can hold an idea as a handle and every loop closes in real time. In S3, shaping an idea is neuro-symbolic programming. I have completed two systems, Dream Brush and Parametric Diffusion, and a screen IDE, Spaceship. On this base I will build Puck Grammar, a tangible neuro-symbolic language; Put That There Again, a tabletop IDE that takes in ideas through Braindump; an actuated puck table on which the AI can move and resist handles; and Sable, a tangible audio synthesizer. Comparing these systems with chat, using the Creativity Support Index (CSI) and measures of agency, ownership, and joy, I test whether S3 preserves agency, enhances creativity, and sparks joy.

Introduction

Symbolic computing is ending. We have entered the age of semantic computing.
— John Maeda

I agree, with one reservation: meaning without symbols gives the hand nothing to hold. Without that hold, today's AI fails people in three ways. First, it is indirect. Users must cross a gulf of envisioning between what they intend and a prompt that anticipates what the model can do [1]. Second, it is pliant. Language models tend to agree with whatever users assert [2], so users have nothing to push against. Third, it is monistic. Generative AI can make each person more creative while it narrows what people make as a group [3]. In all three ways, people lose creativity, agency, and joy.

Each of these problems has an older answer. Against indirectness, cybernetics [4], [5] and Tangible Bits [6] placed the human in a tight feedback loop with the machine. Clark et al. call the human-led form of such a loop machine-in-the-loop: the person leads, the machine suggests, and the person keeps control of the result [7]. Against pliancy, Ingold describes a craftsperson who works with a material that resists and responds, so that form emerges in the making, not from a prior specification [8]. Against monism, constructionism [9] and multi-user tangible tables [10], [11] offer many hands, many objects, and many paths to an idea.

AI could not use these answers because AI was slow. That is changing. Image and video models are approaching interactive frame rates [12], [13], speech models now listen and talk at once [14], and models that track, decide, and write programs are getting faster too. When latency falls below what people can perceive, a series of requests becomes a closed loop. This thesis assumes that real-time AI will arrive soon, and asks what we should build for it.

Speed alone is not enough, because a faster chat is still chat. I propose to give thoughts handles. To hold a thought is to bind it to a symbol that a hand can move: a parameter, a token, a puck. The S3 architecture names three nodes: the somatic node, which is the body; the symbolic node, which holds what code interprets; and the semantic node, which holds what a model interprets. When people shape an idea through these nodes, they write a neuro-symbolic program whose values are meanings. This gives the thesis its central hypothesis:

When people work with AI through the symbolic node, and every loop closes in real time, they will keep more agency, create more, and feel more joy than when they chat with AI in turns.

More than productivity is at stake. When we delegate our thinking to machines, we risk giving away our freedom, our creativity, and our judgment. I want to give people better ways to think with machines, so that they can protect, expand, and share the human experience of being alive.

Contribution

  1. The S3 architecture, toward a paradigm for human-AI interaction. It defines three nodes; the edges between them, including binding between symbols and meaning; the loops they form; and an operational definition of real time.
  2. Puck Grammar, a tangible neuro-symbolic language. People arrange pucks to express verbs such as steer, blend, bind, and lock, and the verbs generalize across domains.
  3. Two IDEs and a runtime: Spaceship (done) on screen; Put That There Again on a table, with Braindump for input; and an actuated puck table on which the AI can move and resist handles.
  4. Applications: Dream Brush [15] and Parametric Diffusion (done) for images, Sable for audio, and Tangible Co-ideation for design.
  5. Empirical evidence and design recommendations on how S3 affects creativity, agency, and joy, compared with chat.

Related Work

Direct Manipulation and Real-Time Interaction

Each major step in interaction design made something live. Sutherland's Sketchpad let a person draw and constrain geometry with a light pen and see the result at once [16]. Engelbart and English's 1968 system let people manipulate text, links, and views in a live workspace [17]. Shneiderman named this pattern direct manipulation: "rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose impact on the object of interest is immediately visible" [18]. Hutchins, Hollan, and Norman explained why it works: it shortens the distance between what people intend and what they do [19]. In S3 terms, Direct Manipulation closed the loop between body and symbol, but it reached only data that already had a symbolic form, such as pixels, geometry, and files.

Researchers have measured how fast such a loop must be. Within about 0.1 s, a response feels instantaneous; within 1 s, people keep their flow of thought but lose the feeling of operating directly on the data; within 10 s, they keep their attention [20], [21]. Direct touch demands more: Jota et al. found that users could not tell 1 ms from 10 ms of latency, but performed worse at 25 and 50 ms [22].

Tangible Media

Tangible interfaces carried these handles into the physical world. In Bricks, Fitzmaurice, Ishii, and Buxton let people grasp virtual objects through physical blocks, with both hands [23]. In Tangible Bits, Ishii and Ullmer gave digital information a physical form [6], and in their token-and-constraint framework they explained how physical tokens act as handles for digital data [24]. Sensetable and Audiopad tracked wireless pucks on a table, each standing for an object or a parameter [10], [25]. The Actuated Workbench then moved such pucks with electromagnets, so that the computer could move a handle as well as read it [26]. Illuminating Clay let people sculpt terrain while the system projected analyses onto it [27], Radical Atoms imagined materials that change form with digital state [28], and the reacTable made the puck table a popular instrument [11].

Recent work in the group brings generative AI into this lineage. In Tangible Co-ideation Across Time, a Tangible Media Group project I contribute to, people prompt AI through physical objects, such as figurines whose position tunes AI expert personas [29], and revive legacy design sketchnotes with conversational AI agents [30]. The project page reports that tangible interaction increased how much people spoke and how much agency their ideas kept relative to AI contributions [31]. I have not reviewed the data, so I treat this report only as prior evidence.

Interface Agents and Their Critics

AI took the other path. Since ELIZA, people have reached machine intelligence by exchanging text [32]. In the 1997 debate on direct manipulation vs. interface agents, Pattie Maes argued that "we cannot just add more and more sliders and buttons," so people must delegate some work to agents; Shneiderman answered that users need predictability and must stay responsible [33]. Shneiderman later asked for high human control and high automation at once [34]. S3 puts the two sides on different edges. Interface Agents connect the body directly to meaning and skip the symbolic node, so they can express much but let users predict little.

Bolt's Put-That-There came closest to joining the two. Its users spoke and pointed, and the system resolved that and there through gesture, but it understood no meaning [35]. LLMs have the opposite problem: they understand meaning but cannot resolve that and there.

Critics of chat propose alternatives. Lee imagined dragging one sentence onto another to interpolate between ideas [36]. Litt et al. argue that software should support direct manipulation first and use AI to complement a malleable environment [37]. Studies of sycophancy [2] and homogenization [3] show that chat is pliant and monistic as well as indirect. Clark et al.'s machine-in-the-loop writing tools leave the writer in control of the result, the stance this thesis takes, but they still deliver suggestions in discrete turns [7].

Neuro-Symbolic Methods and Generative Control

On the symbolic ↔ semantic edge, AI researchers have long combined the two kinds of representation. Neuro-symbolic AI joins learning with symbolic reasoning [38], and neurosymbolic programming builds programs from both neural and symbolic parts [39]. I apply this idea at the interface: I bind symbolic handles to a neural model and let people manipulate them. DirectGPT comes closest, because it lets users edit an LLM's output through direct manipulation instead of retyped prompts [40]; but its users still work turn by turn.

Research on conditioning supplies candidate bindings. ControlNet conditions images on spatial input [41], Concept Sliders turn semantic attributes into continuous knobs [42], and RAVE, DDSP, and differentiable wavetable synthesis expose structured parameters for audio [43], [44], [45]. Pipeline-level batching makes such models fast enough for a loop [12], though most reported speeds for 2025–2026 systems are vendor claims that this thesis does not depend on.

Making, Material, and Creative Computing

Ingold argues that most accounts of making are hylomorphic: the maker conceives a form and imposes it on inert matter [8]. He proposes the textile as a better model, in which the maker follows and joins the flows of the material and the form emerges along the way. Turn-based prompting is hylomorphic: the user writes the form as a prompt, judges the result, and writes the prompt again. A real-time model with handles behaves more like a material, one that the user can work and that pushes back. Schön [46], Kirsh and Maglio [47], Papert [9], and Csikszentmihalyi [48] agree: people think, learn, and enjoy their work by acting on materials that answer back. Creative coding adds an aesthetic tradition. Krueger's Videoplace made real-time embodied interaction an expressive medium [49], Levin described an "inexhaustible, infinitely variable, dynamic" audiovisual substance [50], and Lieberman treats code itself as a creative medium.

Cybernetics and Human-AI Loops

Wiener defined cybernetics as the study of control and communication through feedback in animals and machines [4]. Licklider predicted that humans and computers would be "coupled together very tightly" and would think together in real time [5]. Clark and Chalmers argue that a mind can extend into external resources that are reliable and trusted [51]. Latency therefore matters to cognition, because a slow, intermittent loop is a poor candidate for extension. Clark has since asked whether generative AI will be "mind-extending" rather than "mind-replacing" [52], and Pataranutaporn studies how human-AI systems can support human flourishing and agency [53].

The S3 Architecture

S3 has three nodes, and each node is defined by what interprets it. The somatic node is the body: hands, voice, gaze, and posture. The symbolic node holds what deterministic code interprets, such as parameters, geometry, puck positions, and programs. The semantic node holds what a model interprets, such as meaning in an LLM or a diffusion model. By this definition, a typed prompt is semantic input.

Three edges connect the nodes. The somatic ↔ symbolic edge is Direct Manipulation, through GUIs, tangible interfaces, screens, and haptics. The somatic ↔ semantic edge is Interface Agents, through vision, speech, and chat models. The symbolic ↔ semantic edge is binding, which this thesis develops: parametric prompts carry symbols into a model, and detection, decision APIs, and code execution carry meaning back out as structure. Binding thus gives people structured reasons and reasoned structures. Each node also loops on itself.

Real-time AI speeds up every edge. Tracking and recognition speed up somatic → symbolic, decisions and program synthesis speed up semantic → symbolic, and generation speeds up semantic → somatic. Along these edges, a handle is a full path: a symbol, bound to meaning, that the body can reach. The systems in this thesis run three loops:

  1. Fast loop (somatic → symbolic → semantic → symbolic → somatic). The user moves a handle and watches the meaning change. This loop must run in real time, and it works like Kahneman's System 1 [54].
  2. Slow loop. The user brings in an idea as speech, gesture, or sketch; the model proposes handles; and the user inspects, rebinds, or discards them. This loop may take seconds, like System 2.
  3. Actuated loop (semantic → symbolic → somatic). The AI moves or holds a puck, and the user pushes back.

I define real time as at least 24 frames per second of visible output and no more than 100 ms from input to perceptible response [21]. I treat this bound as a minimum, because direct touch demands more [22]. Where current models are too slow, I will stand in for them with a Wizard-of-Oz or simulated-latency layer, and I will vary latency as an experimental condition.

Completed Work

In Dream Brush, people steer image generation with calligraphic marks on a near-real-time canvas. A vision model reads each character, and the stroke's bounding box selects the region to repaint, so one gesture carries both meaning and place [15]. Because generation still takes seconds, a "drying" animation masks the wait, and the system shows honestly how far near-real-time falls short of real time.

In Parametric Diffusion, people control image generation in real time by directly manipulating an explicit symbolic structure over a diffusion model. I built and showed it as an art project, so I have no user feedback yet and make no claims here about its effect on users.

In Spaceship, a screen IDE, people steer an LLM's text generation in real time through spatial metaphors: locks, a slingshot, a force field, a signal scanner, and vector steering. A video of Spaceship drew about one million views on Instagram. Because commenters did not use the tool, I treat their responses only as an informal probe. Many saw it as control ("That's actual user control over model behavior"); others asked for another use ("I want this for coding, learning and research"), which moved this thesis toward idea work and programming. Spaceship's metaphors now seed Puck Grammar.

Proposed Systems

Puck Grammar is a tangible neuro-symbolic language. People write a program by arranging pucks, which a camera tracks: position, proximity, and rotation map to verbs such as steer, blend, bind, and lock. A computer-vision prototype already runs. I still need to extend the verbs, test whether they compose and abstract, and settle on a notation that people can learn.

Put That There Again is the tabletop IDE, and it absorbs my earlier Papershop prototype. Unlike Bolt's original [35], it understands meaning: people speak and point at ideas on paper or on pucks, and the system binds those ideas to handles. People bring ideas in through Braindump, which captures voice and gesture quickly. Because Put That There Again shares a backend with Spaceship, the studies can compare screen and table directly.

The actuated puck table is the runtime. A PCB coil array moves passive magnetic pucks, so the table can move any object that carries a magnet. The table builds on Sensetable and the Actuated Workbench [10], [26], and aims to be scalable, modular, silent, and 2.5D. With it, the AI closes the third loop of S3: it proposes by moving a handle and resists by holding one. Later, the table could also support Tangible Co-ideation, with persona figurines that move on their own.

Sable applies S3 to sound. People shape a tangible terrain by hand, and the terrain drives a wavetable synthesizer; neural audio models [43], [44], [45] can bind the terrain's shape to timbre. Sable exists today as a web sketch.

Research Questions

Symbolic backbone

  1. Which handles best support semantic manipulation? What makes a puck better than a slider, and what changes when a handle moves or resists on its own?

Perceptual feedback from simultaneous prompting and generation

  1. What does real time change? Compared with turn-based prompting, how do real-time loops through the symbolic node affect creativity, agency, ownership, and enjoyment, and how do these depend on latency?
  2. How should ideas get in? What multimodal input lets people bring in rich context quickly?

A new language for shaping ideas

  1. What are the generalizable verbs of idea shaping? Which operations (steer, blend, scale, bind, pin, branch, merge) carry across domains, and do they compose and abstract?
  2. What happens socially? How do communication and collaboration change when several people share the same handles?

Design Process

I follow Ishii's vision-driven approach, in which a long-term vision, not current technology, drives the work. I build each system as a sequence of rapid prototypes and test them early with small groups of creators, in the creative-coding spirit of daily, playful sketching. I treat joy as a design criterion as well as an outcome: Dream Brush unexpectedly became a social drawing game, and I will look for similar unplanned uses in the tangible systems.

Evaluation

Hypotheses. Compared with people who prompt in turns, people who use S3 will report (H1) more creativity support, (H2) a stronger sense of agency and ownership, and (H3) more enjoyment. (H4) These differences will shrink as latency exceeds the real-time bound.

Measures. I will measure creativity support with the Creativity Support Index [55], which combines six weighted factors (exploration, expressiveness, immersion, enjoyment, results worth effort, and collaboration) into one score out of 100. I will also measure baseline agency with the Sense of Agency Scale [56], pilot unvalidated items for task agency and ownership, and measure workload with NASA-TLX [57]. Logs, think-aloud sessions, and interviews add qualitative data. I will evaluate Puck Grammar in two ways: with CSI, for the creative work it supports, and with a cognitive dimensions analysis [58], for its quality as a notation. An optional probe tests judgment: I will plant a flaw in the AI's output and record whether participants catch it. In the qualitative analysis, I will listen for whether participants describe themselves as the "manager of a machine" or the "author of a craft," in the sense of Ingold's maker [8].

Studies. All study designs are proposals, subject to IRB approval and committee feedback.

  • Study A, images and audio (Dream Brush, Parametric Diffusion, Sable). Participants use (a) text prompts, (b) handles at native latency, and (c) handles at simulated lower latency.
  • Study B, idea shaping (Spaceship, Put That There Again, Puck Grammar). Participants develop an idea in a research or ideation task using (a) LLM chat, (b) Spaceship's on-screen handles, (c) passive pucks, and (d) actuated pucks, if the table is ready (RQ1, RQ4).
  • Study C, collaboration (optional). Small groups shape ideas together on Put That There Again while I observe how they communicate (RQ5).

Limitations. Simulated latency cannot fully substitute for truly real-time systems. CSI was designed to evaluate a single tool, so I must match tasks carefully when I compare systems. Novelty and order can inflate enjoyment, especially with tangible hardware. To counter these effects, I will counterbalance conditions, build strong baselines, and look specifically for where the new interfaces perform worse.
